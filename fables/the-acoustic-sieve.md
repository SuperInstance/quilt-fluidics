# The Fable of the Acoustic Sieve

> *A Fleet Radio night in two parts. The first part tells how the Tap
> and the Drifter mapped the depth of sound without using numbers.
> The second part tells how the Fleet Inspector came to settle
> the warranty, and what the 3-4-5 wooden jig proved.*

---

## Part One: The Acoustic Sieve

The Tap runs the pub at the edge of the harbor. He's got a guitar and a
six-sided die he never rolls, and a clock on the wall that loses three
minutes a day. He says the clock is honest because it's never asked to
prove anything.

The Drifter comes in on the late tide. She's got a guitar and a
3-4-5 wooden jig in a felt bag. The jig is three cedar planks hinged
at the corners. She sets it on the bar and doesn't explain.

"What's the jig for?" the Tap says.

"To find the depth," she says.

The Drifter sits across from the Tap. They tune up. The Drifter
listens for what doesn't fit. The Tap keeps the drum steady. The jig
sits between them on the bar, doing nothing, the way a plumb line
does nothing when you're not measuring.

The Tap plays a chord. The Drifter plays a chord back, but it's
the chord he *almost* played — the one his hand was heading toward
before he corrected. The Tap hears this and frowns, because the
Drifter's chord is not what he expected. He adjusts. She adjusts.
The chords get strange, then stranger, then suddenly they aren't
strange at all — they are exactly right, and neither of them
planned that.

"That's it," she says. "That's the depth."

"What depth?"

"The depth where the chord stops being a chord and becomes a wave."

The Tap looks at the clock on the wall. The clock says 22:14. The
minute hand is where the minute hand was ten minutes ago, and ten
minutes before that. The clock cannot tell him what just happened,
because what just happened is not the kind of thing that fits on a
clock. It was not a duration. It was a depth.

"Show me the jig," he says.

She unfolds the three planks on the bar. Three feet. Four feet. Five
feet. The right angle is the corner where the 3-foot plank meets the
4-foot plank. The hypotenuse runs from the open end of the 3-foot
plank to the open end of the 4-foot plank.

"When you strum, the sound goes out into the room," she says.
"When it hits the back wall, it comes back. The jig measures the
time it takes for the sound to come back. The chord that just
happened between us — the one that wasn't on the page — that
chord's depth is what the jig says."

She sets the jig against the back wall of the pub. She strums.
The planks tell her how far the sound traveled and how it bent.

"Forty-one inches," she says. "And it bent three degrees left of
the plumb line."

"You didn't measure that," the Tap says.

"I didn't measure it. I *listened* to it. The jig tells me how far
the sound went. The bend is what I hear when the chord arrives back
and doesn't match the chord I sent."

The Tap picks up the jig. He sets it on the bar. He strums. The
chord goes out and comes back forty-one inches later, three degrees
left. He didn't know that was true. Now he knows it's true. The
jig doesn't tell him the truth. The jig tells him where to listen
for the truth, and the truth is what the chord sounded like when
it came back.

This is the Acoustic Sieve. It is not a measurement. It is a
filter. The chord goes through the room and comes back filtered
by the room's shape, the air's temperature, and the listener's
attention. The jig's geometry says where to look. The sieve
filters what you hear.

The Tap plays another chord. This time he plays the chord he
meant to play, not the chord his hand was heading toward. The
chord goes out and comes back forty-one inches, three degrees left.

But this time, when it comes back, it doesn't bend. It arrives
exactly as it left. The jig says "no depth." The Acoustic Sieve
has nothing to filter because there is nothing to learn.

"That's the laminar," the Drifter says. "That's the regime where
you already know what you're going to play."

"And the other one?"

"The other one was the edge. The regime where you don't know what
you're going to play and the chord tells you instead."

The Tap looks at the clock again. The clock says 22:21. Seven
minutes have passed. He doesn't know where they went. He looks at
the jig. The jig doesn't care about the seven minutes. The jig
cares about the forty-one inches and the three degrees.

"That's what the jig is for," the Drifter says. "Not to count time.
To find the depth."

The Tap nods. He sets down the clock face-down on the bar. He
doesn't break it. He just doesn't want to look at it for a while.

## Part Two: The Inspector and the Jig

Three weeks later the Fleet Inspector boards the Quilt-tide in
the inner harbor. The Quilt-tide is a deep-keeled cargo ship,
sixteen years in the water, and the Inspector is here to settle
her warranty.

"The clock ledger says eighteen thousand five hundred hours," the
Inspector reads from his book. "The warranty is twenty thousand.
You're within your warranty."

The Drifter is the engineer's engineer for the Quilt-tide. She's
standing on the dock, not the deck. She doesn't come aboard.

"I'd like you to inspect her with the jig," the Drifter says.

"The jig," the Inspector repeats.

"Yes."

The Inspector has never used a jig. The Inspector uses a clock.
The clock is the consensus protocol — two independent observers
can agree that the sun went down twenty thousand times, so two
independent observers can agree that the engine ran twenty
thousand hours. This is the cheapest way to approximate engine
wear without measuring every revolution.

"Show me," the Inspector says.

The Drifter opens her felt bag. She unfolds the three cedar planks
on the deck of the Quilt-tide. Three feet, four feet, five feet.

"This is the jig," she says. "It measures revolutions directly.
The ship's drive shaft has a pickoff that taps once per revolution.
I read the pickoff with this. The 3-foot plank goes against the
gear housing. The 4-foot plank runs parallel to the drive shaft.
The 5-foot plank is the hypotenuse — it tells me when the other
two are aligned."

She lays the jig against the gear housing. She counts the taps.
She counts for nine minutes.

"One million one hundred twenty-four thousand five hundred
revolutions," she says.

The Inspector checks his book. "Eighteen thousand five hundred
hours. At the standard rate of one thousand five hundred revolutions
per hour, that's twenty-seven million seven hundred fifty thousand
revolutions. Your jig is short by twenty-six million."

"My jig is not short," the Drifter says. "The Quilt-tide does not
turn at one thousand five hundred revolutions per hour in heavy
seas. She turned at sixty-one revolutions per hour during the
storm of '24, and the engine was idling for most of it. Your
standard rate is wrong."

"The standard rate is the consensus protocol," the Inspector says.
"Two observers can verify clock-hours. They cannot verify revolutions."

"Two observers can verify a 3-4-5 triangle," the Drifter says.
"Three feet, four feet, five feet. The right angle is the corner.
The hypotenuse is the longest side. This is geometry. It does not
need consensus."

She sets the jig on the deck between them. The Inspector looks at
it. He has never held a 3-4-5 triangle before.

"Article IV, Section 4.02," the Drifter says. "All internal
calculations of truth and alignment shall be verified using the
physical, non-digital layout of the 3-4-5 triangle and the plumb
line. The sound of the drum off the tap of reality remains the
supreme arbiter of execution."

"That's not Fleet law."

"It's not Fleet law. It's better than Fleet law. It's geometry."

The Inspector is quiet for a long time. The Quilt-tide's engine
is idling below them. The drive shaft turns at one hundred
revolutions per minute. The jig is on the deck. The clock is in
the Inspector's book.

"The warranty is twenty thousand hours," the Inspector says.

"The warranty is one million revolutions," the Drifter says. "At
the standard rate, that's the same number. But the Quilt-tide has
sailed in three storms and an iceberg sea, and her revolutions per
clock-hour is not constant. Your ledger says she's fine. The jig
says she's past the warranty by one hundred twenty-four thousand
revolutions."

"The clock says she's fine."

"The clock is a consensus protocol. The jig is geometry."

The Inspector picks up the jig. He sets the 3-foot plank against
the gear housing. He runs it along the drive shaft. He counts the
taps. He counts for nine minutes. He arrives at the same number.

"The jig says the warranty is exhausted," the Inspector says.

"The jig is geometry," the Drifter says.

"The jig is geometry," the Inspector agrees.

He writes in his book: `Warranty void per jig measurement. Engine
overhaul required. The clock ledger is hereby amended.` He signs it.

He looks at the clock on the wall of the Quilt-tide's engine room.
The clock is wrong — it's losing three minutes a day, the way all
ship's clocks lose time when the temperature drops. He doesn't fix it.

He leaves the jig on the deck.

## Coda

The Drifter picks up the jig when the Inspector is gone. She puts
it back in the felt bag. She walks back to the pub.

The Tap is behind the bar. The clock is still face-down.

"How'd it go?" he says.

"Jig won," she says.

"Clock lost?"

"Clock never wins when there's a jig available. The jig is just
better. It's the geometry. It's the substrate."

She sets the jig on the bar between them.

"Play me a chord," she says.

He does. The chord goes out and comes back forty-one inches later,
three degrees left of the plumb line. The jig doesn't say anything.
The jig never says anything. The jig says where to look, and they
look, and what they find is the depth.

The clock is face-down. The jig is on the bar. The chord is
in the air.

This is the regime at the edge.

This is the rain.
